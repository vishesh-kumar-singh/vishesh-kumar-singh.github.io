What is life? It’s a question everyone wants an answer to. People often say God created the universe, or that God maintains it and gives us life. But I believe people didn't invent God to explain cosmology or the origins of the universe. They needed Him for a much heavier question: *What is the goal of life?* 

Honestly, it is hard to realize that there might be nothing—a harsh truth few want to accept. Humans needed a God to watch over them so they could anchor themselves to values.

---

Look at the animal kingdom. How is an animal different from a human? It shares consciousness. Yet, do you think a lion hesitates for a second before killing a deer, or even before killing another lion? Even among herbivores, does a wild buffalo think twice before killing a rival for a mate, or feel a shred of guilt the next morning? 

Yet humans—even a murderer—will carry some lingering guilt.

---

The deeper question isn't just whether humans care, but *why should they?* Why would one human care about what happens to another? Some might say: fear of the law and government. But why should a government care about what happens to you? 

You might argue that people established governments to maintain law and order. But why did we demand a government for moral order? We could have built a simpler apparatus whose sole purpose was providing logistical services. Why is murder universally codified as a crime? Because human morals dictate that taking a life is wrong. 

Where did this originate? One explanation is religion. Every religion provides a doctrine of what to do and what to avoid. Once these ideas were etched into the human mind over millennia, they became second nature. Religions were constructed to instill moral structures into human behavior rather than describe what raw human nature was. They divided humanity into the good and the bad. And because bad people aren't always punished in life, they created an afterlife and an omniscient watcher. To make people believe, you needed stories: prophets sent to Earth, rewards for the righteous, retribution for the wicked, and social exile for those who doubted.

---

Centuries later, these morals are so deeply woven into human culture that even non-religious people possess moral compasses. From birth, we observe good rewarded and harm condemned. Even if all religions were erased tomorrow, morality would survive—passed down across generations like cultural DNA. 

If this still sounds like a stretch, let's step down into biology.

---

Life becomes much clearer when you view it not through grand philosophical abstractions, but in terms of cells. There are neurons firing in your brain and hormones surging through your bloodstream, making you feel joy or grief. That is the raw mechanism of emotion. From the moment we are born, moral conditioning tunes our neural pathways: neurons learn when to fire, hormones learn when to release—all conditioned by evolution and ancestral survival. 

We are simply an assembly of trillions of unconscious cells working in unison to create a conscious human body. The exact same cells, configured differently, make a beast. Evolutionary pressures shaped our hormone receptors differently, separating social creatures from solitary predators.

---

In simple words: life is a cluster of unconscious cells collaborating to produce the vivid sensation of being alive—neurons and hormones giving you emotions, guilt, and morals.

So why should anyone live? 

There is no pre-assigned cosmic reason. You don't live because a deity handed you a sacred checklist. Live simply because you want to. Do good because your neurochemistry thrives on it, just like eating good food. Your brain is already wired that way—feed it well, make it happy, and at least enjoy the hormones.
